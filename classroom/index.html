      Graphs

      About the math

      Chip Fire is built on the chip-firing game: fire an orb and it sends one chip along each of its edges. That one simple rule turns up all over mathematics.

      Combinatorics

      In 1991 Björner, Lovász and Shor showed that the order of the fires never changes where a game ends up. That is why, in Chip Fire, only how many times each orb fires matters.

      Algebra

      Every graph carries a hidden finite group, its sandpile group. Its size is the number of spanning trees of the graph, counted by Kirchhoff’s theorem of 1847. In the mod modes, a board hides extra “do-nothing” patterns of fires exactly when the mod divides that number.

      Statistical physics

      In 1987 the physicists Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld used the same rule to model sand piling up and sliding in avalanches. Their sandpile model became a famous example of self-organized criticality: systems that drift to a tipping point on their own.

      Algebraic geometry

      The Integers mode is the dollar game. In 2007 Baker and Norine used it to prove a Riemann–Roch theorem for graphs, a twin of a classical theorem about curves. One consequence: if the chips add up to at least edges − orbs + 1, the board can always be cleared. This opened a bridge to tropical geometry that is still being explored.

      Still unsolved

      • Sandpile groups. For many families of graphs, even well-known ones like the hypercubes, the exact structure of the sandpile group is still not fully known.
      • Avalanches. Physicists predict exact power laws for the sizes of sandpile avalanches, but relatively little has been proven.
      • Patterns. The picture above was only proven to have a limiting shape in 2013, by Pegden and Smart. Explaining all of its fractal structure is active research.
      • Gonality. The fewest chips that can pay off a debt anywhere on a graph is its gonality. Computing it is NP-hard, and Baker’s conjecture that it is at most (genus + 3)/2 is proven only in special cases.
      • New variations. Games like the mod modes here are far less studied, so there is room for new discoveries, by students too.

      Further reading: Caroline Klivans, The Mathematics of Chip-Firing (2018); Scott Corry and David Perkinson, Divisors and Sandpiles (2018); and the Wikipedia pages on the chip-firing game and the abelian sandpile model.

      How it works

      Fire to zero

      Tap an orb to fire it. It loses one chip for each edge, and each neighbor gains one.

      Numbers run 0 to 4 and wrap around: one more than 4 is 0, and one less than 0 is 4. Make every orb 0.
